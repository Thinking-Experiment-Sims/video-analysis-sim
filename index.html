💡 Why Linearize Parabolic Motion?

In physics, fitting a parabola $y(t) = \frac{1}{2}at^2 + v_0t + y_0$ contains three dependent parameters $(a, v_0, y_0)$. Simply graphing $y$ versus $t^2$ does not produce a straight line unless the initial velocity $v_0 = 0$.

General Kinematics: y(t) = ½at² + v₀t + y₀

🎯 The Discovery Method: Avoiding Dependence on v₀

How can students isolate acceleration $a$ without needing $v_0 = 0$ at the release point?

Look at the apex (peak) of the position-time curve! At the apex $(t_{\text{apex}}, y_{\text{apex}})$, the instantaneous vertical velocity is exactly zero:
v_y(t_apex) = 0

Shifted Vertex Form:
[y - y_apex] = ½a · (t - t_apex)²

Let Y' = (y - y_apex) and X' = (t - t_apex)²:
→ Y' = Slope · X' where Slope = ½a

Clicking "Linearize: y vs (t-t_apex)²" on the Graphs tab automatically detects the apex of your tracked points and plots this linearized model! The slope equals ½a (or -4.9 m/s² for free fall), yielding acceleration directly.

About Vectors: The "Vectors" toggle draws arrows scaled to instantaneous velocity $(\vec{v} = v_x\hat{i} + v_y\hat{j})$. In projectile motion, note how the horizontal arrow remains constant while the vertical arrow shrinks to zero at the apex and points downward on descent!
